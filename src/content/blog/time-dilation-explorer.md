In everyday life, time feels like a universal metronome ticking at the exact same rate for everyone, everywhere. Move fast enough, however, and that intuition breaks down. **Time dilation** is the physical phenomenon where a clock moving relative to an observer ticks more slowly than a clock at rest in the observer's own frame. At ordinary human speeds the difference is imperceptible—even aboard NASA's Parker Solar Probe, screaming around the Sun at 192 km/s (`0.00064c`), a clock loses only about 18 milliseconds over an entire day. But as a spacecraft's velocity `v` approaches the speed of light (`c = 299,792.458 km/s`), the slowdown grows dramatically according to the Lorentz factor `γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)`. At `0.866c`, ship time runs at half the rate of Earth time (`γ = 2×`); at `0.9999c`, a single year aboard the ship spans more than 70 years on Earth.

The path to discovering time dilation began with a puzzle in electromagnetism. James Clerk Maxwell's equations predicted that light travels at a fixed speed `c`, and in 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley famously tried—and failed—to detect any change in the speed of light as the Earth hurtled through space along its orbit. Hendrik Lorentz and Joseph Larmor worked out the mathematical transformations needed to explain why light's speed always measured the same, and in 1905 Albert Einstein took the decisive step in his theory of **special relativity**: instead of treating the math as an electromagnetic quirk, he showed that the speed of light is a fundamental constant in every inertial reference frame, and therefore *time itself* must be relative. The cleanest way to see why is Einstein's **photon light clock** thought experiment. Imagine a pulse of light bouncing vertically between two mirrors inside a spaceship. To an astronaut aboard the ship, the photon simply travels straight up and down. To a stationary observer watching the ship fly past, that same photon has to chase the moving mirrors along a longer diagonal, sawtooth path. Because the speed of light `c` cannot increase to compensate for the longer diagonal path, each bounce genuinely takes longer to complete in the stationary observer's frame.

What started as a thought experiment was soon measured directly in the lab and the sky. In 1938, Herbert Ives and G. R. Stilwell confirmed relativistic time dilation by measuring the Doppler shift of fast-moving hydrogen ions. Three years later, Bruno Rossi and David B. Hall measured cosmic-ray **muons**—unstable subatomic particles created high in the upper atmosphere with a rest lifetime of just 2.2 microseconds. Even traveling near the speed of light, most muons shouldn't survive the trip to the ground if their internal clocks ticked at normal speed, yet they arrive at sea level in huge numbers because their lifetimes are stretched by `γ`. In 1971, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating brought the test down to human scale by flying cesium-beam atomic clocks around the world on commercial jetliners and comparing them against stationary reference clocks at the U.S. Naval Observatory, confirming the exact nanosecond shifts predicted by relativity. Today, time dilation is an everyday engineering reality: GPS satellites orbit at ~14,000 km/h and must continuously account for relativistic clock shifts, or navigation on Earth would drift by kilometers every day.

## What the Time Dilation Explorer does

To make those numbers tangible, the newest addition to the site is the [Time Dilation Explorer](/projects/time-dilation)—an interactive special relativity lab where you can dial a spacecraft's velocity from rest all the way up to `0.99999c` and watch two reference frames diverge in real time.

- **Velocity Control & Ultra-Relativistic Zoom.** Because the Lorentz factor stays nearly flat (`γ ≈ 1`) across the first 80% of a linear `0 → c` slider and then shoots toward infinity in the final percent, a standard slider makes the most interesting speeds hard to dial by hand. The velocity panel includes benchmark presets—from the Parker Solar Probe (`0.00064c`) and `0.866c` (`2×` slowdown) up to `0.9999c` (`70.7×`)—along with an **Ultra-Relativistic Zoom** toggle that switches the slider to a logarithmic scale across "the nines" (`0.90c`, `0.99c`, `0.999c`, `0.9999c`, `0.99999c`). Four live telemetry readouts track the Lorentz factor `γ`, the traveler's relative tick rate `1/γ`, physical speed in `km/s` and `mph`, and relativistic length contraction `L/L₀`.
- **Live Dual-Frame Clocks.** Two side-by-side cards compare a **Stationary Observer (Earth)** at rest against a **High-Speed Traveler (Spaceship)** moving at your chosen velocity. Each card pairs an SVG analog clock and a centisecond digital stopwatch with a biological/mechanical heartbeat indicator pulsing at 60 BPM in proper time (which drops to `60 / γ BPM` for the ship as observed from Earth) and a starfield that stretches into relativistic streaks as `v → c`. You can pause, reset, or crank the simulation rate up to `10×`, `1 min/s`, or `1 hr/s`.
- **Photon Light Clock & Lorentz Curve.** Below the clocks, a split-screen Canvas 2D visualizer animates the photon light clock side by side: vertical bounces in the ship's rest frame on the left, and the longer diagonal sawtooth path (with Lorentz-contracted mirrors) seen by the stationary observer on the right. Beside it, an adaptive Lorentz curve plots `γ` against `v/c` with a live crosshair at your current speed, automatically expanding its vertical scale up to `250×` when you push into ultra-relativistic territory.
- **Cosmic Journey & Twin Paradox Calculator.** The bottom panel turns the current velocity into a mission planner. You can specify a one-way voyage by **ship time (`Δt'`)**, **Earth time (`Δt`)**, or **destination distance in light-years**, or click one of five cosmic destinations: Alpha Centauri (`4.37 ly`), Sirius (`8.6 ly`), TRAPPIST-1 (`39.5 ly`), the Galactic Center (`26,000 ly`), or the Andromeda Galaxy (`2.54M ly`). At `0.99999c` (`γ ≈ 223.6×`), a trip across the 2.54 million light-years to Andromeda takes 2.54 million years on Earth, but just over 11,300 years aboard the spacecraft.

## How it works

Like the [Speaker Box Designer](/projects/speaker-box) and [Arkanoid](/projects/arkanoid), the Time Dilation Explorer runs entirely on the stack the site already ships—React 19, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, inline SVG, and native HTML5 Canvas 2D—with **no new dependencies and no backend**.

The architecture splits cleanly into two layers:

1. **Pure physics & verification (`src/lib/relativity/`).** All of the relativistic formulas, linear and logarithmic slider mappings (`β(t) = 1 − 0.1 × 10^(−4t)`), and cosmic journey solvers live in pure TypeScript modules with no UI dependencies. A standalone verification harness (`verify.ts`) asserts 30 numerical checks against the feature plan: exact benchmark values (`β = √3/2 → γ = 2.000000`), inverse `β ↔ γ` and slider round-trips, and the equivalence between time dilation in Earth's frame and length contraction in the traveler's frame (from the ship's perspective, the distance to a star is contracted to `d' = d / γ`, so crossing it at `v = βc` takes `Δt' = d / (βγ)`—the exact same proper time derived from Earth's clock).
2. **Keeping 60 fps out of React state.** React renders the velocity controls, telemetry cards, and mission calculator whenever you adjust a slider or pick a destination. The continuous 60 fps simulation—the analog clock hands, digital stopwatches, heartbeat pulses, starfield streaks, and bouncing light-clock photons—runs inside a single `requestAnimationFrame` loop that writes directly to DOM/SVG refs and `ResizeObserver`-scaled canvases. In the photon light clock, the ship and observer phases are integrated through independent accumulators (`dφ_obs = dφ_ship / γ`), so dragging the velocity slider smoothly bends the angle of the photon's diagonal sawtooth path in real time without ever jumping or resetting the animation.
