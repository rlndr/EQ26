A few times a month, somewhere over the planet, a rock hits the atmosphere hard enough for US Government sensors to record the flash. NASA publishes those events, along with every asteroid passing close by and a list of objects with any chance of hitting us in the coming centuries. [Near Earth](/projects/near-earth) puts all three on one page, as past, present and future.

## What it does

- **Past.** A 3D globe replays 38 years of fireballs, each flaring as the timeline reaches it. The largest by far is Chelyabinsk in 2013, at 441 kilotons, nine times the next on the list.
- **Now.** A chart of every asteroid passing within 0.05 au over the next 60 days. Pick one and watch its actual path past the Earth and Moon, from JPL Horizons.
- **Future.** All 2,242 objects on JPL's Sentry list, on a chart and a table, with the odds in plain words.
- **An energy ladder** runs from Hiroshima to the dinosaur impact, and you can add any fireball or risk object to it.

## The honest part

The most useful thing I learned building this is how much uncertainty hides in the numbers. One asteroid passing next month sits at a nominal 3.1 lunar distances, but could be anywhere from 1.2 to 24.8, so the chart draws that range instead of a single dot.

The risk list is also more reassuring than it sounds. Only two objects out of 2,242 rate above "no likely consequences" on the Palermo scale, the nearest of those is centuries away, and 3,597 objects have already been cleared from the list as new observations came in. Some of the highest odds belong to objects a few metres across, which would break up high in the atmosphere.
